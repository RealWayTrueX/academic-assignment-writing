# Catalogue of machine-writing tells in academic prose

Each tell is listed with what it looks like, why it reads as machine output, and the fix. The examples come from two course documents (a methodology section and a software-tools report) before they were revised; the "after" versions are in `../examples/before-after.md`.

| # | Tell | What it looks like | Why it reads as machine | Fix |
|---|------|--------------------|-------------------------|-----|
| 1 | Meta-discourse template | "This document specifies the methodology ...", "This document states which software ...", and a closing "This document specified a hybrid research path ...". | The same "This document + verb" frame opens and closes every piece; a person starts with the question or the thing. | Open with the research question, the object or the claim. Close with what is now known and what comes next, not with a restatement of the opening. |
| 2 | Counting tic | "Three requirements decide the software." "Python is kept for three reasons. First, ... Second, ... Third, ..." "Four reasons justify ...", "One feature exists only in R." | Nearly every paragraph announces a count and then enumerates; the structure is a nested checklist rather than an argument. | Write the items as connected prose. Keep labelled lists only where the labels are used later (A1 to A5, R1 to R15, risks (1) to (6)). |
| 3 | Telegraphic fragments | "The rule is therefore: use Python first." "Inference: clustering by tract." "Exposure model." as a run-in label followed by noun phrases. | Over-compressed, low-perplexity text; abstracts stretched into body text. | Give every body sentence a subject and a verb. Run-in labels are fine in a risk list; the sentences after them are not. |
| 4 | Uniform rhythm | Mean sentence length around 16 to 20 words with a small spread; short declarative plus colon plus list, again and again. | Low burstiness is the single most reliable statistical signature of generated text. | Let a complex idea run to 35 to 45 words with subordinate clauses, then follow with a short sentence. Check SD/mean in the audit (aim for at least 0.45; careful human prose is often near 0.5 or above). |
| 5 | Low lexical diversity | "reproducible", "fixed seed", "ordinary laptop", "no licence is needed", "public data" five or six times in two pages. | Signature phrases are recycled instead of varied. | Rotate synonyms (rerun, regenerate, replicate), merge repeated statements, cut the third occurrence. |
| 6 | Over-symmetric structure | Every section ends with the same shaped sentence; abstract, introduction and conclusion mirror one another word for word. | Humans drift; templates do not. | Give each section its own closing move: a consequence, a limit, a question for the next section. |
| 7 | Zero or inconsistent author voice | No first person anywhere, then one stray "my effect"; no judgment words at all. | Generated text avoids commitment; a lone "my" shows the seam. | First person singular for choices and judgments, hedged honestly ("I believe", "likely", "unfortunately"); impersonal for facts. One voice from abstract to conclusion. |
| 8 | Parenthetical patches | "(not mortgages)", "only about imports, not totals", "an equilibrium quantity, so the sign of my effect is not known in advance". | Reads like a correction bolted on after generation. | Rewrite the sentence so the qualification is part of it. |
| 9 | Template paragraph openers | "The exposure model ...", "The risk set holds ...", "The panel is balanced ...", "The estimates below serve ..." | Each paragraph begins with subject + copula + definition; parallelism becomes monotony. | Rotate openers: a question ("Which buildings belong in the risk set?"), a concession, a cause, an example, a back-reference. |
| 10 | Machine lexicon | delve, crucial, pivotal, tapestry, testament, moreover, furthermore, additionally, notably, leverage, utilize, comprehensive, intricate, multifaceted, underscore, foster, landscape, realm, paradigm, seamless, "it is worth noting", "plays a vital role", "not only ... but also". | High-frequency in generated text, rare in careful technical prose. | Replace with the plain word (main, important, also, use) or delete. |
| 11 | Em-dashes and triplets | "exposure — the potential penalty — is ..."; three adjectives or three clauses by reflex. | Punctuation and grouping habits of generated prose. | Commas, colons, parentheses or a new sentence; two items or four when that is what there are. |
| 12 | Over-claiming verbs | "confirms", "establishes", "validates", "proves" for results that merely agree with expectation. | Confidence that the evidence does not support. | "is consistent with", "suggests", "shows"; pilot numbers "check the design; they are not findings". |
| 13 | Boilerplate emphasis | "In conclusion, it is clear that ...", "It is important to note that ...", "This highlights the importance of ...". | Filler that carries no information. | Delete, and say the specific thing. |
| 14 | Borrowed rule wording | "The benchmark places Python on the 'similar' side of the rule." | Mimics the decision rule's text instead of narrating the decision. | "Python fell on the 'similar' side of my decision rule." |

## What not to remove

High density of specific numbers (25,245 records to 2,854 buildings, MDE = 0.53 points, 96 seconds, 3.7 GB, seed 20261018), statute sections (NYC Admin. Code 28-320.6, 1 RCNY 103-14), numbered equations that the text refers to, labelled robustness checks, complete appendix tables, script names. These hard details are what makes a document read as the work of someone who did it. Style edits leave them exactly as they are.
